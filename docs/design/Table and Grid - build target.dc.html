The table and the grid, T — build target

This rules the Collection screen’s table and grid views, in the record page’s vocabulary. It is drawn from Code’s survey of the build, and there is no build of these designs, so this file shows no specimen. It is prose until Code builds it, and every ruling about how something looks is provisional until its build step captures it.

T.1 · Why these views are designed, and why here

The table and the grid are designed in the record page’s vocabulary, not left as the app’s chrome. This is Adam’s ruling: they should take the record page’s styling so the app is one thing. It supersedes §W.27’s position that they stay chrome, ruled only where they contradict a drawing, and §W.27 carries the withdrawal.

They have their own target because they are a different page from the shelf, with no rail and a heading and count of their own, as the nav and the modal have theirs. What they share with the shelf is §W.24’s search field and its row of view names, and this file points at them and does not restate them. The views’ own search field goes, and the band’s takes its place. Their band is §W.24’s two-row form at every width, search above the view names. Search takes 443, the measure the record page gives its title, wherever the width less insets exceeds it, and the width less insets below that: 350 at 390, 280 at 320, and 443 from 483 up. It is a figure the app already owns. Withdrawn within §T.1: one filter row holding GENRE and LABEL, 24 apart, with LABEL taking a second row where the two do not fit at 320. Below the view names, these views add their filters, one line each, which the shelf does not have, so their band is taller than the shelf’s and the Collection page’s length differs between them: the shelf’s 971 at 390 is not theirs. Content sits at the 20 inset, with no centred column, and the filter lines and their lists are held to 443, search’s measure, so the band’s elements share one. These were Code’s choices and are ratified. At 768 and up the 443 column is the control column, heading, count, band and filter block, and the figure stands beside it. The measure keeps its figure, which is the record page’s title measure, and its reason changes: it was one measure for elements with nothing beside them, and it is now the width of one of two columns. It is not widened, because the filter box opens at 443 below the block and the figure’s alignment depends on the block. The four filter lines and Sort are 44 each, the floor. Any gap between them beyond that is the build’s, not ruled, and step 111 reports and removes it. Withdrawn within §T.1: the band at 126.5 and the closed filters with the Sort row at 224. 224 was stale from step 98, which made the Sort control 44, and 126.5 went false at step 100, which made search 44. Measured on the real collection after step 100: the band is 136.5 at every width, the filters closed with the Sort row 232. Open, the lists are 1,408, 572, 88 and 44, for genre, label, store and tag, and since the panel covers, they no longer add to the page’s length. The table’s page is 1,786, 1,903 and 1,401, and the grid’s 2,702, 2,439 and 1,345, at 390, 320 and 1440.

The table is for finding, and the shelf is for looking. Once the collection outgrows a screen, the table is how a record gets found, sorted and compared. So where the record page’s figures were set for one record on a page and would cost the table its density, the table takes its own figure inside the same vocabulary, and says so.

T.2 · The vocabulary they take

The type is the record page’s ladder, and the seven sizes the survey found become three: 40 for the heading, 13 for everything a reader reads, and 11 for every label. §4.1 rules those sizes, and its labels are mono, uppercase and tracked .10em, by §G.1. Nothing is set at a size the record page does not use.

Colour is ink, paper and the label colour, and nothing else. Withdrawn by §T.6: colour in these views as ink, paper and the label colour and nothing else. §T.6 lets a record’s own tint into the ornament. Add record loses its oxblood and is ink, by §3’s reason: it is the same on every visit, and nothing constant takes colour. The hairline is §3’s, at the value §W.27 sets, and the surface that sinks under a pointer is §W.27’s step below paper.

No corner is rounded. The survey found radii of 2, 2.4 and 3, and they go. §9.3 rules no radius for its controls. Square is not ruled app-wide, so these views make the rule, by extending §9.3’s “no radius”. Code reports that it lives only in the control vocabulary, §9.3, the nav target and §M.6, that SPEC.md says nothing, and that the app’s stylesheet defines a 3px radius. That radius is shared, so squaring it everywhere would silently restyle manage, stats and the record form, which have had no pass. The change is scoped to these views, and the other screens take it when they are ruled. Withdrawn by §T.6: the square scoped to these views, with the shared 3px radius left on other screens until they are ruled. §T.6 gives them a colour and heading pass, so the reason for scoping, that they had no pass, is spent.

Every control a finger is expected to hit meets §9.3’s 44, boxed or by overlay, as the maintaining notes rule app-wide. Code’s survey of the real collection found 69 controls at 390, all under it. The number grows with the genre and label counts. An earlier draft gave 28, from seeded test data, and it was corrected before Code first took this target, at 5d123ee.

T.3 · The heading, the count, search and filters

The count sits directly above the heading, as an 11 label over a 40, which is §4.1’s adjacency: small first, large beneath, nothing between. The heading stays at 40, the size §4.1 gives the artist.

The search field is the band’s field, by §W.24, per §T.1. It has no button. Search is submitted on Enter, and a SEARCH button would add a control to one view’s copy of a band it shares with the shelf.

Filters are a disclosure, not a set of chips always shown. There are four, GENRE, LABEL, STORE and TAG, in SPEC.md §10’s order, each on a line of its own, because the reason for the disclosure reaches all four, and the closed state’s job is to show what is in force, which one combined line cannot do for four without growing long. Each filter is single-valued, as the build, the address and the API are: its line shows its 11 label, then the one option chosen, in ink, and nothing when none is. Withdrawn within §T.3: several options chosen in one filter, and the count of those that overflow the line. Choosing several would change the records endpoint, which is SPEC.md’s region and a change of capability, not of styling. So the underline on the chosen option is §3’s singular case, the current one of a set, and the extension below is not needed for filters, though it stands. Pressed, it opens its options in a panel beneath its line. Each option is a 44 row with a hairline between, the chosen one marked in ink with §3’s 2px underline 7px below the baseline. Each row keeps its option’s count, as an 11 label right-aligned in the row, so counts align down the list, for the reason §T.4 right-aligns figures. The underline is extended to mean what is in force, one of a set or several of a set: §3 wrote it for the current one, and the table’s sort column and the nav still use it that way, with exactly one marked. A chosen filter is in force in the same sense, so the same mark reads the same way, and a second mark would add vocabulary for one difference a reader does not need, how many can be on. Pressed again, or Escape, closes it. One filter is open at a time, and choosing an option closes its list, which follows from a filter holding one value. Withdrawn within §T.3: the open list pushing the page down, on the ground that a covering list would have to move its own trigger. Adam ruled it a drop-down like the nav menu, and the reason does not hold: the menu’s trigger never moves, because its panel covers only what lies beneath the header’s row. Step 97b built the push-down, so the entry is owed. An open filter covers what lies beneath its own line on opaque paper, as §G.8’s menu covers what lies beneath the header’s row, and the page beneath does not move. The mechanics are §G.8’s: opaque paper, a tap on the panel’s paper below the list or Back closes it, the page held in both directions, with step 86’s scrollbar compensation, and Escape and one filter at a time as above. One close set at both widths: a tap anywhere outside the list’s rows (the sheet’s paper, the inset beside a row included, or the page beside the box); CLOSE, a §9.3 control in the panel’s top row; Escape; Back; choosing an option; or a press on another filter’s line, which switches. An option row’s hit area ends at the row’s drawn edge. A press that closes the panel does nothing else, so a tap outside the box that lands on a table row closes the box and does not open the record. A tap closes and a drag scrolls: a press closes only if it lifts within the platform’s tap slop of where it landed, without a scroll having begun. The list’s scrolling area spans the panel’s full width, inset included, so a drag that starts on the inset scrolls the list. The fork is in the panel’s shape, not its behaviour. When the page moves far enough that the last filter line leaves the window, the open panel closes. The panel belongs beneath its line, and with the line gone it would be an open list with no trigger, which nothing reads as a filter. Holding it at the window’s top, the interim Code built, draws a panel detached from what opened it. Bringing the lines back would fight the move that took them away. Since step 107 holds the page against a finger and §T.3 holds it in both directions, what can still move the page is the reader’s own reach outside the panel: find-in-page, focus moving onto the page, scroll restoration. Each means the reader has gone to the page, so the panel closes, as a close is honoured from any state. Closing does nothing else, and focus returns to the filter’s line only if that line is in the window; otherwise focus stays where the move put it. The cost: a find-in-page with a filter open closes the filter. Keyboard scrolling of the page while a panel is open is held by §T.3, so if it moves the page, that is a defect, not this case. No entry is owed: “beneath the last filter line” stays true wherever a panel is open. Each filter now agrees with G.8/push-down, which withdrew pushing for the menu, and §T.3 cites it as precedent. Where the options exceed the space, the list scrolls vertically within the panel, as the menu’s does. That is not the sideways scrolling ruled out above, which hid options in a row behind a gesture nothing invites: a vertical list that runs past the panel’s edge invites the scroll it needs. Genre’s options, 1,408 tall, always do. Withdrawn within §T.3: the panel covering what lies beneath its own line, which hid the filter lines below it. With Genre open, three lines were under opaque paper, so a reader changing two filters paid an extra tap or not depending which they opened first, and a press on a line they could no longer see could not be designed for. Withdrawn within §T.3: the panel covering what lies beneath the last filter line at every width. Adam judged the full-width sheet wrong for a desktop, where it uses none of the page’s space; step 102 built it, so the entry is owed. Withdrawn within §T.3: the box never less than 176 tall, which draws a mostly empty box for a short list. Opening a filter in a short window then costs the reader nothing they keep. A close caused by the line leaving the window leaves the page where the reader’s own move put it. T.4 · The table

This file rules how the table’s columns look, not which columns it has, which stay as built until the survey lists them.

Withdrawn within §T.4: rows 44 tall at every width. Rows are at least 44 tall and as tall as their content, with a hairline between them and no fill. 44 is the floor, because a row is one link to its record. Under a genre filter a row carries a third line saying why the record matched, and below 768 a line with the label and condition. The cost is density: at most about seventeen rows on an 800-high screen. Withdrawn within §T.4: the table as one long page of 200 records. The table pages at 50. The table keeps its density elsewhere: in 13 type with tabular figures, in columns set to their content, and in no padding inside a row beyond what centres the type. Finding is done by sorting and searching, not by scrolling. If Adam judges the rows too loose from a capture of a full page, the rows become a stated exception to the floor, with the reason: a table of records is read and compared, and its rows are a list, not a row of separate controls. The floor cannot be met by overlay there. Rows are adjacent, with only a hairline between them, so a 44 overlay on a shorter row would reach into both neighbours, and the neighbours’ overlays would collide, leaving a band where a tap is ambiguous. An overlay works for §W.24’s links only because they have a gap to share. Adam judged it from the full-page capture in docs/captures/table-98/: 44 reads fine, so the floor holds as built and the exception is not taken. No entry is owed, because nothing was built against the exception.

The Record column, title and artist, takes the table’s slack, 518 of 1,400 at 1440, because it is what a reader reads. Text is left-aligned and figures right-aligned in tabular figures, so a column of prices or years can be compared down its length. The column headers are 11 labels. The column the table is sorted by is marked as §3 marks the current one of a set: ink, with the 2px underline. Its direction is shown by an arrow after the label, ↑ or ↓, in ink. A header is a sort control. The table keeps §T.5’s Sort control at every width, because two of the five orders have no header to carry them at any width: Date bought has no column, and Artist has none of its own, being the Record cell’s second line. The columns that drop at narrow widths, Label and Cond., are not sort orders, so no order is lost with them. If Date bought ever gets a column, the control’s scope shrinks to Artist.

The header row stays at the top of the viewport while the table scrolls. Comparing figures 150 rows down needs to know which column they are in. §G.6 rules that the app’s header scrolls away, and step 74 pinned it with a test, so the table’s header row sticks at the top of the viewport itself. Code is confirming that the build still matches. If the app’s header is sticky there, the table’s header sticks 53 below the top, under it, and that is a coupling to §G that this section states, not one it assumes. Under a pointer a row sinks to §W.27’s surface, and keyboard focus is §M.6’s ring, drawn inside the row because the table clips nothing outside it.

Which columns survive at 390 and 320 is not ruled. It needs the columns and their measured widths, which are in the survey and not in this file.

T.5 · The grid

Each record is its cover in a square, by §33’s rule for the record page: cropped to fill within the 95% bound, fitted beyond it, and held back until its shape is known. A record with no cover shows the frame §6 and §5.3 rule, at the square’s size. One rule for a cover everywhere is what makes the grid the record page’s.

Under each cover, the title is set in 13 ink, and the artist beneath it as an 11 label, with nothing else. The whole cell is one link to its record. So the grid does not say why a record matched a genre filter, as the table does. The reader chose the filter, the grid’s job is the cover, and a line on every cell at two to ten a row would clutter it. The cost is the parent-genre case, where a record matched through a parent such as Hardcore and the grid withholds that. It is accepted.

Covers are 24 apart, inset 20 a side from the window, at least two to a row, and as many as fit at 160 or wider. At 390 that is two covers of 163, and at 320 two of 128, where the two-column minimum wins over the 160. The count changes at named widths, not discovered ones, measured at step 99: 568, 752, 936, 1120, 1304, 1488, 1672 and 1856, from three columns to ten. Withdrawn within §T.5: the grid reaching eight, nine and ten columns at 1488, 1672 and 1856. §T.6 caps the content at 1,400, which holds seven columns of 160 with 136 to spare. So the grid stops at seven, from 1304, and its covers grow to about 179 at 1,400. Step 99 measured and carried the three, so the entry is owed. Each is 40 plus n × 160 plus (n − 1) × 24, a constant 184 apart. At 568 a cover drops from about 251 to 160. The 24 is §G.3’s gap and the 20 is §W.24’s inset. In the grid, Sort is a §9.3 control whose label names the current order, such as SORT · YEAR ↓, because the grid has no column headers to carry it.
