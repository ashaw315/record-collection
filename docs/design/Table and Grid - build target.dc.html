Sections in this file are §T.1 onward. T is for table. It appears as no section prefix, file key or letter-number id in any target, handoff or the withdrawal list I hold. Code confirms it is free of every file key, section prefix and id, and that the only §T ids anywhere are this file’s own.

The table and the grid, T — build target

This rules the Collection screen’s table and grid views, in the record page’s vocabulary. It is drawn from Code’s survey of the build, and there is no build of these designs, so this file shows no specimen. It is prose until Code builds it, and every ruling about how something looks is provisional until its build step captures it.

T.1 · Why these views are designed, and why here

The table and the grid are designed in the record page’s vocabulary, not left as the app’s chrome. This is Adam’s ruling: they should take the record page’s styling so the app is one thing. It supersedes §W.27’s position that they stay chrome, ruled only where they contradict a drawing, and §W.27 carries the withdrawal.

They have their own target because they are a different page from the shelf, with no rail and a heading and count of their own, as the nav and the modal have theirs. What they share with the shelf is §W.24’s search field and its row of view names, and this file points at them and does not restate them. The views’ own search field goes, and the band’s takes its place. Below the view names, these views add one row of their own, holding both filters, GENRE then LABEL, 24 apart, which the shelf does not have. So their band is one row taller than the shelf’s, and the Collection page’s length differs between them: the shelf’s 971 at 390 is not theirs, and the build step pins their own.

The table is for finding, and the shelf is for looking. Once the collection outgrows a screen, the table is how a record gets found, sorted and compared. So where the record page’s figures were set for one record on a page and would cost the table its density, the table takes its own figure inside the same vocabulary, and says so.

T.2 · The vocabulary they take

The type is the record page’s ladder, and the seven sizes the survey found become three: 40 for the heading, 13 for everything a reader reads, and 11 for every label. §4.1 rules those sizes, and its labels are mono, uppercase and tracked .10em, by §G.1. Nothing is set at a size the record page does not use.

Colour is ink, paper and the label colour, and nothing else. Add record loses its oxblood and is ink, by §3’s reason: it is the same on every visit, and nothing constant takes colour. The hairline is §3’s, at the value §W.27 sets, and the surface that sinks under a pointer is §W.27’s step below paper.

No corner is rounded. The survey found radii of 2, 2.4 and 3, and they go. §9.3 rules no radius for its controls. Square is not ruled app-wide, so these views make the rule, by extending §9.3’s “no radius”. Code reports that it lives only in the control vocabulary, §9.3, the nav target and §M.6, that SPEC.md says nothing, and that the app’s stylesheet defines a 3px radius. That radius is shared, so squaring it everywhere would silently restyle manage, stats and the record form, which have had no pass. The change is scoped to these views, and the other screens take it when they are ruled.

Every control a finger is expected to hit meets §9.3’s 44, boxed or by overlay, as the maintaining notes rule app-wide. Code’s survey of the real collection found 69 controls at 390, all under it. The number grows with the genre and label counts. An earlier draft gave 28, from seeded test data, and it was corrected before this target had any drop taken.

T.3 · The heading, the count, search and filters

The count sits directly above the heading, as an 11 label over a 40, which is §4.1’s adjacency: small first, large beneath, nothing between. The heading stays at 40, the size §4.1 gives the artist.

The search field is the band’s field, by §W.24, per §T.1. Whether its button stays depends on what search does, so Code reports it. If the field filters as the reader types, the button goes. If search must be submitted, the button is a §9.3 control labelled SEARCH.

Filters are a disclosure, not a set of chips always shown. Each filter sits in the filter row of §T.1: its 11 label, then the options chosen, in ink, and nothing when none is. Where the two do not fit one row at 320, LABEL takes a second, and the build step reports the width at which it does. Pressed, it opens its options as a list in flow beneath the line, pushing the page down. Each option is a 44 row with a hairline between, chosen ones marked in ink with §3’s 2px underline 7px below the baseline. The underline is extended to mean what is in force, one of a set or several of a set: §3 wrote it for the current one, and the table’s sort column and the nav still use it that way, with exactly one marked. A chosen filter is in force in the same sense, so the same mark reads the same way, and a second mark would add vocabulary for one difference a reader does not need, how many can be on. Pressed again, or Escape, closes it. One filter is open at a time. It pushes the page down rather than covering it, unlike the nav menu, deliberately. G.8/push-down withdrew pushing for the menu because the menu replaces the screen’s navigation. A filter list is content in the page’s own flow, and pushing keeps its trigger exactly where the reader’s finger already is, so one press closes it without scrolling back. A covering list would have to move its own trigger.

The reason is that the options grow with the collection. Code measured inline chips on the real collection at 44 a line: genre took 9 lines at 390 and 13 at 320, and label 5 and 6. That is 664 at 390 and 904 at 320, more than a phone’s screen before a single record. The 44 floor is not the cause: at today’s 30.5 a line it is still about 426. The cause is showing every option always. Genres were 6 in the seed and are 32 now, so any design whose height scales with them fails at some count. An earlier draft ruled inline wrapping, and it is withdrawn here as a draft: this target has had no drop taken.

Closed, the filters cost one line each, at any count. Open, the list is as long as its options, and that is accepted, because the reader has asked for it and closes it with one press. Within the open list nothing scrolls sideways, for the reason that ruled out sideways chips: a row that scrolls hides its options behind a gesture nothing invites. Where the chosen options are too many for their line, the line shows as many as fit and then a count, such as +3, in the label colour, so the line never wraps.

T.4 · The table

This file rules how the table’s columns look, not which columns it has, which stay as built until the survey lists them.

Rows are 44 tall at every width, with a hairline between them and no fill. A row is one link to its record, so the floor reaches it. The cost is density: about seventeen rows on an 800-high screen, and a 200-record table is a long page. The table keeps its density elsewhere: in 13 type with tabular figures, in columns set to their content, and in no padding inside a row beyond what centres the type. Finding is done by sorting and searching, not by scrolling. If Adam judges 44 too loose from a capture of a 200-record fixture, the rows become a stated exception to the floor, with the reason: a table of records is read and compared, and its rows are a list, not a row of separate controls. The floor cannot be met by overlay there. Rows are adjacent, with only a hairline between them, so a 44 overlay on a shorter row would reach into both neighbours, and the neighbours’ overlays would collide, leaving a band where a tap is ambiguous. An overlay works for §W.24’s links only because they have a gap to share.

Text is left-aligned and figures right-aligned in tabular figures, so a column of prices or years can be compared down its length. The column headers are 11 labels. The column the table is sorted by is marked as §3 marks the current one of a set: ink, with the 2px underline. Its direction is shown by an arrow after the label, ↑ or ↓, in ink. A header is a sort control. The table keeps §T.5’s Sort control at every width, because two of the five orders have no header to carry them at any width: Date bought has no column, and Artist has none of its own, being the Record cell’s second line. The columns that drop at narrow widths, Label and Cond., are not sort orders, so no order is lost with them. If Date bought ever gets a column, the control’s scope shrinks to Artist.

The header row stays at the top of the viewport while the table scrolls. Comparing figures 150 rows down needs to know which column they are in. §G.6 rules that the app’s header scrolls away, and step 74 pinned it with a test, so the table’s header row sticks at the top of the viewport itself. Code is confirming that the build still matches. If the app’s header is sticky there, the table’s header sticks 53 below the top, under it, and that is a coupling to §G that this section states, not one it assumes. Under a pointer a row sinks to §W.27’s surface, and keyboard focus is §M.6’s ring, drawn inside the row because the table clips nothing outside it.

Which columns survive at 390 and 320 is not ruled. It needs the columns and their measured widths, which are in the survey and not in this file.

T.5 · The grid

Each record is its cover in a square, by §33’s rule for the record page: cropped to fill within the 95% bound, fitted beyond it, and held back until its shape is known. A record with no cover shows the frame §6 and §5.3 rule, at the square’s size. One rule for a cover everywhere is what makes the grid the record page’s.

Under each cover, the title is set in 13 ink, and the artist beneath it as an 11 label, with nothing else. The whole cell is one link to its record.

Covers are 24 apart, inset 20 a side from the window, at least two to a row, and as many as fit at 160 or wider. At 390 that is two covers of 163, and at 320 two of 128, where the two-column minimum wins over the 160. The count changes at named widths, not discovered ones: a third column of 160 first fits at 568, where a cover drops from about 251 to 160. Each later width is where one more 160 and its 24 fit. The build step reports every width at which the count changes, up to 1920, and they are written here from that report. The 24 is §G.3’s gap and the 20 is §W.24’s inset. In the grid, Sort is a §9.3 control whose label names the current order, such as SORT · YEAR ↓, because the grid has no column headers to carry it.
